## Executive Summary

- A useful way to think for manufacturing SMEs facing capacity gaps across operations: instead of asking "should we hire more people or buy more software," ask "which specific step in this workflow could have an AI employee supporting it, with a clearly assigned task and authority."
- McKinsey Global Institute's research ("A Future That Works," 2017), analyzing more than 2,000 activities across 800 occupations, produced an important finding: only about 5% of occupations could be fully automated with the technology available at the time, but about 60% of occupations have at least 30% of their constituent activities that are technically automatable. This is evidence for a core principle: **automation/AI should be viewed at the activity (task) level, not the job level.**
- Applied to workflow: instead of asking "could this position be replaced by AI," the better question is "which step in this position's work is a good fit for an AI employee to support."
- An AI employee is the best fit for steps that are repetitive, high-volume, or involve preparing/synthesizing information before a person decides — not the entire scope of a position.
- The boundary between "AI support" and "AI replacement" comes down to what proportion of a position's activities get handed to AI — and that boundary should be designed deliberately, not left to form randomly.

## Opening

Many small and mid-sized manufacturers face a familiar problem: several points in their operations need more people, but hiring is slow, expensive, and a suitable candidate isn't always available. The natural question that follows: "can AI fill this gap?"

The useful answer isn't a yes/no for the entire position — it's a different frame: **instead of asking whether AI could replace a position, ask which step in that position's workflow could have an AI employee supporting it.** This is the difference between viewing a job as an indivisible block, and viewing it as a sequence of steps, each with a different degree of fit for AI.

---

## What an AI Employee Means in Workflow

Following on from the previous article on AI participants, an "AI employee" here isn't a general-purpose chatbot — it's an AI participant assigned **one or a few specific steps** within a position's workflow, with a clearly defined task, authority, and evidence trail, as covered in article 5.17.

The key difference from the common framing ("AI replaces employee X"): an AI employee doesn't take over an entire position — it takes over **some of the activities that make up that position**. For example, an accounts receivable clerk has several activities: reconciling invoices, tracking overdue payments, contacting customers for payment reminders, handling disputes, and periodic reporting. An AI employee can support invoice reconciliation and overdue-payment tracking well — but handling a dispute with a customer still requires human judgment and relationship management.

This is precisely the central observation in McKinsey Global Institute's "A Future That Works" (2017) research: after analyzing more than 2,000 constituent activities across 800 different occupations, the study estimated that only about 5% of occupations could be fully automated with the technology available at the time, but about 60% of occupations have at least 30% of their constituent activities that are technically automatable. In other words: **automation/AI rarely replaces an entire job — it typically changes part of it.**

**Implication:** Viewing an AI employee at the activity (task) level, rather than the job level, is a far more grounded approach than asking "will this position be replaced by AI" — a question that usually produces one of two extreme reactions: excessive fear, or unrealistic expectations.

---

## Which Steps Are Appropriate

Not every step in a process is a good fit for an AI employee. Three traits help identify the best candidates:

**1. Highly repetitive, high volume.** Steps that occur often and accumulate significant time — even if each individual instance isn't complex — are good candidates. Reconciling documents, compiling periodic reports, and reviewing data against known patterns all fall into this group.

**2. Requires preparing or synthesizing information before a person decides.** This is the "recommend" function covered in article 5.10 — the AI employee prepares context, relevant data, and an initial proposal, so the person can focus on the part that genuinely needs judgment, instead of spending time gathering information themselves.

**3. Success/failure can be clearly defined.** If an activity can be clearly assessed as "done correctly" or "not" (figures reconcile or don't, classified into the right category or not), assigning it to an AI employee and verifying the result is far easier than for an activity where "right" or "wrong" depends mostly on relationships or situational judgment.

Conversely, steps that require relationship-building, negotiation, or judgment in ambiguous, unprecedented situations — following the "nonprogrammed decisions" distinction covered in article 5.15 — should continue to be handled by people, at least for now.

---

## Conditions for It to Work

For an AI employee to genuinely operate effectively at a specific step, three conditions are needed, inherited from earlier articles in this series:

**1. That step needs to be clearly separated from other steps** in the process — as covered in articles 5.9 and 5.14, it needs to be clear what the AI employee proposes, who authorizes it, and who executes, so the boundary of responsibility doesn't blur.

**2. There needs to be enough historical data or clear rules** for the AI employee to perform that activity reliably — if the step still sits in the "needs heavy reasoning" zone of Simon's continuum (article 5.15), the AI employee will need tighter oversight, or shouldn't be deployed there yet.

**3. There needs to be an identity and authority governance mechanism** for that AI employee — as a participant with its own identity, a clear scope of authority, and an evidence trail, exactly as covered in article 5.17.

Missing any one of these three conditions, putting AI into a specific step usually creates more operational risk than value — even when, technically, the model is perfectly capable of performing that activity.

## The Boundary Between AI Support and AI Replacement

This is the question many CEOs and COOs genuinely care about, often left unspoken: "will an AI employee ultimately cost my employees their jobs?"

Based on the analysis above, the honest answer is: **it depends on what proportion of a position's activities get handed to AI — not a yes/no answer for the whole position.** If a position has 70% of its activities in the repetitive, high-volume, clearly right-or-wrong category — and an AI employee takes over most of that share — the remaining human role at that position changes significantly, concentrating on the 30% that requires judgment, relationships, or exception handling.

This isn't "replacement" in the sense of eliminating the position — it's **restructuring that position** around what people do best, while an AI employee handles the repetitive share. For many capacity-constrained manufacturing SMEs, this is often the only realistic way to expand operational capacity without hiring proportionally to every increase in workload.

The important part is that this boundary needs to be **designed deliberately** — clearly deciding which activities go to AI, which stay with people, and why — instead of letting it form randomly through scattered, unplanned AI tool deployments.

## Conclusion

The useful question isn't "which positions will be replaced by AI" — it's "which step in each position's workflow is a good fit for an AI employee to support, and which should continue to be handled by people." McKinsey's research shows this isn't a theoretical question — most real jobs already contain both types of activity, and the greatest value comes from getting the split right, not from picking one extreme or the other.
