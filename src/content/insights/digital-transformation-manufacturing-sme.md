> **Executive Summary**
>
> - "Digital transformation" is a term used so broadly it has lost practical meaning. For manufacturing SMEs, it needs to be redefined in a real operational context — not in the context of large enterprises or in the way software vendors frame it.
> - There are five stages in the digitalization journey — from paper-based operations to AI-assisted operations. Most manufacturing SMEs are at stages 1–2, and the next economically meaningful step is usually not jumping to stage 5.
> - Three most common mistakes: buying software before process preparation, confusing document scanning with real digitalization, and expecting digital transformation to generate value immediately.
> - The right question isn't "should we do digital transformation?" but "where are we in the digitalization journey — and what is the next step with the clearest economic case?"

## Why "digital transformation" has become a meaningless term

Search for "digital transformation" online. Within a few minutes you'll find:

- Software companies explaining why their product is the digital transformation solution.
- Articles quoting failure rates for digital transformation projects — figures that range from 70% to 84% depending on the source, with no consistent definition of "failure."
- Articles about AI, cloud, and Industry 4.0 with more buzzwords than useful content.
- And very little that's genuinely useful for the CEO of an 80-person manufacturing business who wants to understand what the actual next step looks like.

This is the problem with the term: "digital transformation" is used to describe everything from switching to accounting software from paper ledgers to deploying full AI across a supply chain.

When a term describes everything, it describes nothing specific.

This article doesn't attempt to provide a new definition of "digital transformation." Instead, it tries to answer a more practical question: **for a small or mid-sized manufacturing business, what does the digitalization journey look like — and what is the next step with real economic value?**

## Digitalization is not a destination — it's a journey with stages

One of the most common misconceptions about digital transformation is that it's framed as a binary destination: the business "has digitalized" or "hasn't digitalized." Reality is more complex — and more useful when viewed differently.

Digitalization is a continuous journey with distinct stages, each with its own value and each building the foundation for the next.

Here is a practical view for manufacturing SMEs:

---

### Stage 1 — Paper-based operations

Operations primarily depend on paper documents, handwritten records, manual ledgers. Information isn't stored in ways that can be searched or analyzed.

Organizations at this stage: most processes depend on who wrote things down and who remembers. Audit preparation takes weeks because records must be searched manually.

**Meaningful next step:** Begin digitizing the most important records and documents — not scanning to PDF, but bringing them into systems where they can be searched and retrieved.

---

### Stage 2 — Digital documents

Documents have been digitized — Word, Excel, PDF files saved in shared folders. But information is still static and lacks the structure needed for analysis.

Organizations at this stage: many files, hard to find anything. Version control is chaotic. Excel is the de facto operating system. Audit preparation is better than stage 1 but still requires significant effort.

**Meaningful next step:** Build a DMS with version control. Standardize processes so they can be documented accurately.

---

### Stage 3 — Digital workflow

Processes are executed through systems — approvals go through workflows, status is trackable, records are automatically generated as processes are executed.

Organizations at this stage: have a DMS, eQMS, or ERP across some functions. Audit preparation is significantly faster because records are created during operations. However, multiple systems often don't connect with each other.

**Meaningful next step:** Integrate critical systems. Standardize data so it can be analyzed and compared.

---

### Stage 4 — Integrated processes and analyzable data

Critical systems are connected. Operational data can be analyzed over time, by product, by production line. Decisions are based on data rather than intuition.

Organizations at this stage: ERP is operating effectively. Quality data can be analyzed for trends. Important operational questions can be answered without manual consolidation.

**Meaningful next step:** Build organizational knowledge — connect knowledge to data. Prepare for contextual AI.

---

### Stage 5 — AI-assisted operations

AI participates in processes as a participant — not only as a document lookup tool. Organizational knowledge is sufficiently developed for AI to support decisions, detect exceptions, and help maintain operational consistency.

Organizations at this stage: very few manufacturing SMEs are here yet. This is a development direction, not a starting point.

## Where is your business actually?

An important observation before reading further: most manufacturing SMEs are not at the same stage across all dimensions. A single business may:

- Have an ERP but employees still use Excel because the ERP doesn't fit actual workflows (stage 3 in terms of systems, stage 2 in terms of operational reality).
- Have well-digitized quality records but production planning still on paper (stage 3 for QC, stage 1 for production scheduling).
- Have good accounting software but manage inventory in Excel (stage 3 for finance, stage 2 for warehouse).

This is very common — and it means the question "which stage is the business at?" needs to be asked dimension by dimension, not answered with a single response.

## Three most common mistakes in digital transformation

### Mistake 1 — Buying software before preparing processes

This is the most common trap. An organization implements an ERP or management system without standardizing processes first — then is surprised when the system doesn't generate the expected value.

Software doesn't improve a disorganized process. It just runs that disorganized process on a computer instead of on paper. And usually slower, more expensive, and harder to adjust.

Process standardization must come before — or at minimum in parallel with — software deployment.

### Mistake 2 — Confusing document scanning with digitalization

Many organizations believe that once all documents have been scanned to PDF, they've "digitalized." This is not accurate.

Scanned PDFs are image-format information — not searchable by content, not analyzable, not consolidated, and not usable by AI in any meaningful way.

Real digitalization requires structured data — information stored in ways that can be queried, analyzed, and linked to other information.

### Mistake 3 — Expecting immediate value

Digital transformation is a cumulative journey, not a one-time event with immediate results. Many organizations deploy a system, don't see ROI within 3–6 months, and conclude that "digital transformation isn't right for us."

The real value of digitalization typically emerges later — when data is sufficient for trend analysis, when processes are consistent enough to measure improvement, when AI has enough context to operate meaningfully.

This isn't a reason not to start. It's a reason to start correctly — with realistic expectations and a long-term plan.

## A straightforward principle for manufacturing SMEs

Before thinking about technology, answer this question:

*"Without any software, what specific operational problems is the business experiencing — and are those problems information problems (missing data, can't find information) or process problems (undefined processes, inconsistency, not being followed correctly)?"*

If the problem is process: software won't solve it — processes must be standardized first.

If the problem is information: the right software can help — but you need to identify clearly what type of information is missing and why.

From that answer, the next step that actually fits the business becomes clearer — rather than starting with "what software should we buy?"

## A digitalization roadmap appropriate for manufacturing SMEs

There is no single correct roadmap for every business. But several principles help build an appropriate one.

**Principle 1 — Start from the pain point, not the technology**

The pain point is a real operational problem currently generating cost or risk. Examples: audit preparation taking too much time, inconsistent quality across shifts, knowledge disappearing when key people leave.

From that pain point, identify the right solution — which isn't necessarily a large software system.

**Principle 2 — The next step must fit the existing foundation**

A business at stage 1 shouldn't jump directly to stage 4. Each stage builds the foundation for the next — skipping stages is building a house without a foundation.

**Principle 3 — The economic value of each step must be clear**

Every digitalization investment needs a clear answer to: what problem does this solve, what value does it create, and what does it cost? If that can't be answered, it may not be the right step.

**Principle 4 — Progressive approach, not all-or-nothing**

Digital transformation isn't "deploy an entire system from day one." A progressive approach — starting with the smallest unit with clear value, learning from it, expanding gradually — is typically more effective and less risky than a large project.

---

## What is the next economically meaningful step?

Not "what technology should we invest in?" but "which operational problem is currently costing the most — and what is the smallest digitalization step that could begin to address it?"

For some businesses, the answer is: document the five most critical processes that don't yet have written SOPs — no software needed, just time and method.

For others: deploy a simple DMS for document version control — before thinking about eQMS or ERP.

For others still: standardize how quality inspection data is recorded — no new system, just changing how the existing one is used.

These small steps, done correctly and in the right sequence, are real digital transformation — not buying large software or scanning documents.
