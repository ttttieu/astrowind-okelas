By one count, Stalin received around 87 credible intelligence warnings of a German attack during 1940 and 1941. On 22 June 1941, the attack came anyway, against what was still the largest military force in the world. Within a week, two Soviet army groups had been encircled around Białystok and Minsk.

The Red Army did not lack people. It did not lack officers who would later become some of the most capable commanders of the war. It did not even lack information.

What it lacked in those first weeks was harder to see: **the ability for those people and units to see the same reality, and to turn what they saw into coordinated action.**

If you run a manufacturing business with several departments, several systems and several layers of reporting, this story is worth your time. Not because a company is a battlefield, but because the failure mechanism will feel familiar.

## Three layers of failure

**Layer 1 — Losing sight of reality:** Warnings were filtered; commanders lost contact with their own units.

**Layer 2 — Deciding on outdated information:** Orders were issued from an outdated plan, not from the front's actual situation.

**Layer 3 — Losing the ability to adjust:** When people saw problems, they had no safe way to change the plan.

Recovery came from simplifying the structure to match real capability, not from adding more reporting layers.

## A test for your organization

Tick the statements that apply to your company:
- Bad news reaches leadership later than good news.
- Weekly reports rarely surprise leadership, but incidents do.
- Two departments give two different numbers for the same metric.
- In a crisis, information moves mainly through phone calls and key people.
- The annual plan is rarely revised mid-year, even when assumptions change.
- People who spot problems often don't know who to tell.

If four or more apply, the issue is probably not a shortage of talent or tools, but the link between information, decision and action.
