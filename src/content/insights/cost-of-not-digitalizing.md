> **Executive Summary**
>
> - When evaluating digitalization, businesses typically focus on investment costs — software, implementation, training. These are visible and easy to quantify.
> - The cost of staying manual rarely appears in any report — but it's real and accumulates daily: employee time on tasks that could be automated, errors from manual processes, management time handling avoidable problems, and the opportunity cost of slow decisions from information delays.
> - As with the knowledge loss article: no reliable universal statistics exist for these costs. This article provides a framework for CEOs and CFOs to estimate the impact for their own organization.
> - When both sides of the equation are visible — the cost of digitalization investment and the cost of staying manual — many delayed decisions become harder to defend.

---

## Why the cost of not digitalizing isn't counted

![Digitalization investment has its own budget line, while four costs of not digitalizing stay hidden in wages and operations](/assets/images/insights/cost-of-not-digitalizing/cn-01-visible-vs-hidden-cost-en.svg)

There's a structural bias in how businesses evaluate digitalization costs: investment costs (software, implementation, training) appear clearly in budgets and receive careful scrutiny. The cost of staying manual doesn't — it's hidden across many different budget lines or has no budget line at all.

This creates an asymmetry in decision-making: CEOs and CFOs can clearly see "how much digitalization costs" but can't clearly see "how much staying manual is costing." When the current cost is invisible, delayed decisions look safer than they actually are.

## Four types of manual operation costs

![Four types of manual operation cost, from easiest to hardest to measure: employee time, errors, management time and opportunity cost of slow decisions](/assets/images/insights/cost-of-not-digitalizing/cn-02-four-cost-types-en.svg)

### Cost 1 — Employee time on manual tasks that could be automated

This is the most common cost and typically the most underestimated.

Consider a few typical manual tasks in a non-digitalized manufacturing business:

**Report compilation:** An employee spends 2–4 hours each week gathering data from multiple sources, entering it into Excel, and producing a management report. At an average labor cost, 2–4 hours per week × 50 weeks = 100–200 hours per year. Multiply by the number of people doing this, and the figure becomes significant.

**Information searching and waiting:** How much time do employees spend each day searching for documents, asking colleagues, or waiting for information? Even 15–20 minutes per day × 250 working days = 60–80 hours per year per person.

**Duplicate data entry:** The same information entered into multiple systems or forms — a common reality when systems aren't connected.

**Audit preparation:** As analyzed in the audit preparation article: 2–4 weeks of preparation for one audit, with 3–5 people involved, is a genuinely significant annual cost.

This is "invisible cost" because employee time is always assumed to be in use — no one sees it as waste. But when calculated specifically, the numbers are usually surprising.

### Cost 2 — Errors from manual processes

Manual processes have higher error rates than system-controlled processes — this is well-established in quality management literature, though specific rates vary considerably by task type and conditions.

In manufacturing, manual errors can include:

- **Data entry errors:** When information is entered manually into multiple places, entry errors occur. For quality inspection results, a data entry error can lead to an incorrect decision about a batch.
- **Wrong document version:** When documents aren't well version-controlled, employees may execute procedures from outdated documents.
- **Missed steps:** When processes don't have digital checklists or workflows enforcing correct sequence, steps can be skipped — especially under time pressure.

The cost of each error varies enormously: from a few minutes correcting a data entry error to very high costs if an error leads to a non-conforming product or compliance violation.

### Cost 3 — Management time handling avoidable problems

Middle management and CEOs must spend time on:

- **Meetings to consolidate information** that a system could provide automatically: "What are we producing this week, what's the status?"
- **Handling problems from delayed or incorrect information:** When decisions are made based on manually-consolidated information that may be outdated, the probability of a wrong decision or required adjustment is higher.
- **Resolving information conflicts:** When multiple people have different versions of data (stored in separate, unsynchronized Excel files), time spent determining "which number is correct" is a real cost.

Senior management and CEO time is the most expensive in the organization — and the opportunity cost of spending it on these issues rather than strategic decisions is real.

### Cost 4 — Opportunity cost of slow decisions

This is the hardest to measure — and potentially the largest.

When information isn't available immediately and takes 2–3 days to consolidate, many decisions are delayed:

- Quality problems detected later → wider impact before being addressed.
- Operational trends detected later → improvement opportunities missed.
- Slower response to market changes or customer requirements → lost opportunities or degraded service.

This opportunity cost varies considerably between industries and businesses — but in many cases, it's larger than all other costs combined.

## An illustrative scenario — estimating manual operation costs

![Illustrative estimate of the time cost of manual operations for a 100-employee food manufacturer: $6,450 to $10,600 per year, assumed figures](/assets/images/insights/cost-of-not-digitalizing/cn-03-illustrative-scenario-en.svg)

*Important: The scenario and figures below are illustrative assumptions — not case study data or research statistics. The purpose is to demonstrate cost structure so CEOs and CFOs have a framework to estimate for their own organization.*

---

**Context:** Food manufacturing business, 100 employees, at Level 2 in the digitalization journey (digital documents but scattered, primarily running on Excel and email, no DMS or eQMS).

**Estimated annual manual operation costs (illustrative):**

*Report compilation:*
3 employees × 3 hours/week × 48 weeks = 432 hours. At average labor cost, estimate approximately $1,300–$2,200/year (varies with actual wages).

*Information searching and waiting:*
Estimated 20 minutes/person/day × 30 office employees × 250 days = 2,500 hours. Estimate approximately $2,600–$4,300/year.

*Audit preparation (2 audits/year):*
4 employees × 2 weeks × 2 audits = 640 hours. Estimate approximately $1,700–$2,600/year.

*Duplicate data entry:*
Assume 15 minutes/person/day × 15 employees = approximately 600 hours/year. Estimate approximately $850–$1,500/year.

**Total estimated manual operation cost (labor time only): $6,450–$10,600/year**

Not included: error costs, non-conformance remediation costs from manual operations, and opportunity cost of delayed decisions.

---

*Note on these estimates: All figures illustrate cost logic — not statistical data. In practice, actual costs vary considerably by organization size, industry, wage levels, and degree of current manual operation. The goal is to show that manual operation costs are typically much larger than most people recognize — not to provide an accurate benchmark.*

## Why these costs don't appear in reports

As with knowledge loss costs, manual operation costs have no dedicated budget line.

Time employees spend compiling reports isn't recorded as "manual operation cost" — it's recorded as employee wages, allocated to the relevant department. No one separates out "this portion of wages is avoidable waste that could be automated."

This creates bias: digitalization investment costs are immediately visible and specific (X for software, Y for implementation). Manual operation costs are hidden in total labor and operational costs — invisible unless deliberately separated and calculated.

The result: delaying digitalization looks "safe" — not spending money. But in reality, money is being spent every day; it's just not visible.

## How to estimate the cost of not digitalizing for your organization

![Four steps to estimate the cost of not digitalizing: list tasks, estimate time and people, calculate labor cost, estimate error costs](/assets/images/insights/cost-of-not-digitalizing/cn-04-estimate-your-own-en.svg)

Rather than relying on illustrative figures from this article, it's more useful to estimate based on your organization's actual reality:

**Step 1 — List frequent manual tasks:**
Which tasks in the business are currently done manually that could be automated? Report compilation, data entry, information searching, audit preparation, status tracking...

**Step 2 — Estimate time and people:**
For each task: how many people do it and how long does it take each time? How often does the task occur each week or month?

**Step 3 — Calculate labor cost:**
Total time × average labor cost (wages + overhead). This is a number that can be calculated from the organization's actual data.

**Step 4 — Estimate error costs:**
In the past 12 months, how many incidents or problems had wrong, delayed, or missing information as a contributing cause? What was the cost to address them?

The total from these four steps — even before including opportunity costs — often surprises CEOs and CFOs with its scale.

## Conclusion

The cost of not digitalizing doesn't not exist — it just doesn't appear clearly in any budget line.

When CEOs and CFOs begin evaluating the digitalization decision not as "should we spend money on X?" but as "which is the larger cost — digitalization investment or continuing to absorb the hidden cost of manual operations?" — the decision is usually much clearer.
