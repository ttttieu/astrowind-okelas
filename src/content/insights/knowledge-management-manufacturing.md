> **Executive Summary**
>
> - In manufacturing businesses, most important knowledge lives in people's heads, not documents. The experience of handling anomalies, adjusting lines under unusual conditions, and the reasoning behind decisions — these are almost never written down.
> - When an organization depends on individual knowledge without building organizational knowledge, every risk compounds: loss of people, inconsistent quality, inability to scale, and audit failures.
> - Organizational knowledge doesn't emerge naturally as a business grows. It must be built intentionally — through process, systems, and a culture of capturing knowledge.
> - This article examines the problem, the mechanisms, the consequences, and how to address it — not to sell software.

---

## When the best shift supervisor decides to leave

A food processing business, around 80 employees, running two shifts. The morning shift supervisor — call him Michael — had been with the company for nine years. He knew everything: fine details of each production line, how to adjust temperature when humidity shifted, the early signs of the mixing machine before it flagged a fault, why certain raw material batches needed different handling.

None of this was written down anywhere.

When Michael gave three months' notice, management realized the real problem. Not finding a replacement — that was HR's question. The real problem was: *where does what Michael knows go when he leaves?*

The practical answer: a small part would transfer through "handover training" during those three months. Most of it would disappear.

After he left, situations he handled in five minutes would take hours and multiple people — until someone else accumulated enough experience to fill the gap. If they stayed long enough.

*This is a composite illustrative scenario from characteristics common to manufacturing SMEs. Not a case study of a specific organization.*

## What organizational knowledge is — and isn't

![Documentation is text that records information and may be wrong or outdated without the reader knowing; organizational knowledge is the capacity to decide correctly, handle new situations, execute consistently and learn, regardless of who is present.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-01-documents-vs-knowledge-en.svg)

One clarification matters: organizational knowledge is not the same as documentation.

When most businesses think about knowledge management, they think about documents: SOPs, work instructions, forms, reports. Some organizations have large volumes of documentation — yet still experience exactly the problems knowledge management should solve.

The important distinction:

**Documentation** is text recording information. It may be accurate or outdated, currently in effect or superseded — and the reader often can't tell which.

**Organizational knowledge** is the organization's capacity to: make correct decisions, handle new situations, execute processes consistently, and learn from experience — based on what the organization knows and understands, independent of who happens to be present today.

An organization with extensive documentation but knowledge living in people's heads has documents, not organizational knowledge.

When organizational knowledge is built, new employees get up to speed faster, operational quality depends less on who is on shift today, and the organization learns from incidents instead of repeating them.

## Tacit knowledge and explicit knowledge — why this distinction matters

![Explicit knowledge can be written down and passed on through documents such as SOPs; tacit knowledge lives in personal experience, like sensing a machine is off, and often decides quality under non-standard conditions.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-02-explicit-vs-tacit-en.svg)

In knowledge management, there is a foundational distinction between two types of knowledge.

### Explicit knowledge — articulable knowledge

Knowledge that can be written down, read, and transferred through documents.

Examples: incoming materials inspection procedures, product specifications, packaging standards, approved supplier lists.

Explicit knowledge can be documented — and SOPs are the most common form in manufacturing.

### Tacit knowledge — implicit knowledge

Knowledge existing in a person's experience, skill, and judgment — difficult or impossible to transfer through conventional documentation.

Examples: the operator's sense that the machine is behaving unusually before any gauge shows it. How to adjust mixing time when ambient temperature changes. The ability to assess raw material quality through sensory evaluation before laboratory results arrive.

Tacit knowledge is what separates an experienced person from someone new. In manufacturing, it often determines actual quality under non-standard conditions.

### Why this distinction matters in practice

The problem with tacit knowledge isn't that it's hard to transfer. The problem is that most organizations don't realize how heavily they depend on it for critical decisions — and only discover this when the person holding that knowledge leaves.

In a food manufacturing plant, the incoming quality controller may be making acceptance judgments based on eight years of experience — judgments that, if written as documentation, would require pages of contextual explanation. When that person leaves, the replacement will have the documentation. But not the eight years.

Converting tacit knowledge to explicit knowledge — never completely, but meaningfully — is one of the core tasks of knowledge management.

## Why manufacturing SMEs are especially vulnerable

![Four characteristics that make manufacturing SMEs vulnerable to knowledge loss: shift operations, non-standard conditions, compliance requirements and staff turnover.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-03-four-vulnerabilities-en.svg)

Not every industry depends on tacit knowledge equally. And not every organization suffers the same consequences when knowledge is lost.

In manufacturing businesses — particularly SMEs — several characteristics make the knowledge problem especially serious.

**Shift operations — knowledge becomes fragmented**

When a business runs multiple shifts, each shift may develop its own approaches to undocumented situations. Over time, "the morning shift way" and the "night shift way" can diverge significantly — with no one recording differences, and no one knowing which is better.

**Complex products, non-standard conditions**

In many manufacturing processes — particularly food and processing — actual operating conditions differ from ideal conditions in SOPs: raw material quality varies, weather affects parameters, equipment drifts gradually. Handling these variables requires experience — and that experience isn't in documentation.

**Specific compliance requirements**

Organizations with ISO, GMP, or food safety certifications need traceability and audit-readiness requirements beyond documentation — they need evidence that processes were executed correctly. When knowledge about correct execution lives in people's heads rather than systems, compliance becomes a double problem: no evidence is captured, and execution itself may be inconsistent.

**Higher staff turnover**

Manufacturing SMEs often face higher turnover — particularly at operational and technical levels. Each time an employee leaves, knowledge leaves with them. And the real cost is rarely calculated fully.

## The cost of not building organizational knowledge

![Knowledge in people's heads leads to five consequences that accumulate: inconsistent quality, long training, key people leaving, hard audits and inability to scale.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-04-five-consequences-en.svg)

When an organization doesn't build organizational knowledge, consequences don't appear immediately. They accumulate — and usually only become visible when already difficult to address.

### Inconsistent quality

When operational knowledge lives in people's heads, output quality depends on who is working today. This shows up most clearly between shifts, or when experienced employees are absent.

From a customer perspective, this is quality inconsistency that is hard to explain and harder to fix — because the root cause is a knowledge deficit, not a technical defect.

### Long and expensive training periods

When knowledge transfers primarily through verbal instruction and observation, every new employee needs more time to reach independent competence. And experienced employees spend more time training — rather than doing their actual work.

In many organizations, extended onboarding isn't because new employees are slow learners — it's because the knowledge they need isn't organized well enough to learn efficiently.

### Dependency risk when key people leave

This is the most visible consequence — and the most consistently underestimated. Organizations know they're dependent on certain individuals, but don't know the depth, and don't address it until the person gives notice.

By then, it's too late for complete knowledge transfer. Three months' notice isn't enough to transmit nine years of experience.

### Difficult and inconsistent audit preparation

In compliance-regulated environments, audit preparation depends on demonstrating that processes were executed correctly. When knowledge about correct execution lives in people's heads and isn't systematically captured, audit preparation becomes a reconstruction effort — trying to build evidence after the fact rather than summarizing evidence captured during normal operations.

And audit outcomes may vary significantly depending on who leads preparation — not because operations differ, but because knowledge of how to present operations accurately resides in different people.

### Inability to scale

When the operating model depends on individual knowledge, the business cannot expand easily — whether scaling production, adding lines, or opening new facilities.

Each scaling effort requires solving the knowledge problem from scratch. And the solution is typically "find someone with experience" rather than "use the systems we've built to transfer knowledge."

## A knowledge management maturity roadmap — from where you are

![Five maturity levels of knowledge management, from knowledge in people's heads to an organization that learns and improves; Level 3 still does not address tacit knowledge and real operational value starts at Level 4.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-05-maturity-ladder-en.svg)

Knowledge management isn't a software system you buy and install. It is a progression with multiple stages — and the right stage depends on where your organization currently stands.

Five maturity levels provide a useful framework:

### Level 1 — Knowledge lives in people's heads

Most SMEs start here. There is no serious problem when the business is small and founders or core employees are still present. Problems begin when the business grows or staff changes.

Signal: the question "why do we do it this way?" is answered with "ask A" or "we've always done it this way."

### Level 2 — Basic documentation

The organization begins writing SOPs, work instructions, procedures. This is the right direction — but typically produces a large collection of files that no one actually uses.

Common problem at this level: SOPs are written and filed, rarely updated, not integrated into actual operations. Documentation exists but knowledge still lives in people's heads.

### Level 3 — Documents living in a system

The organization has a document management system: version control, defined approvers, clear visibility into which documents are currently in effect. Employees can find the right document — rather than searching email threads or unorganized shared folders.

This is the minimum needed to pass an ISO audit systematically — but it still doesn't address tacit knowledge.

### Level 4 — Knowledge connected to process and context

At this level, knowledge is not only stored but connected: which documents apply to which products, under which conditions, with what evidence. When an incident occurs, it's possible to trace back to decisions made in similar circumstances and understand the reasoning.

This is where knowledge management begins to create genuine operational value — not only compliance value.

### Level 5 — Organizational knowledge that learns and improves

At the highest level, the organization doesn't just capture knowledge — it learns from it: analyzing trends, improving processes based on patterns, detecting improvement opportunities before they become problems.

At this level, AI can participate meaningfully — not as a document lookup chatbot, but as a genuine component of the organization's operational and learning systems.

## The first step isn't buying software

A common mistake when organizations recognize a knowledge management problem is to immediately seek a software tool — an internal wiki, a DMS, or a complex KM platform.

Tools are part of the solution — but they are not the starting point.

The right starting point is an honest assessment: **where is the organization now, and where is the most serious knowledge gap?**

Some questions for self-assessment:

![Four self-assessment questions on dependence on individuals: key people leaving, tracing an incident, new employees looking things up, and audit preparation time.](~/assets/images/insights/kmp-06-four-self-check-questions-en.svg)

If 2–3 of the most important employees left simultaneously, could the organization continue operating normally?

When a quality incident occurs, can the organization identify the cause within one day — or does it take days to gather information from multiple people?

When a new employee joins, can they look up procedures and understand how to work independently — or do they remain dependent on direct guidance for every situation?

When an audit arrives, does preparation take a few days or several weeks?

If most answers reveal heavy dependence on specific individuals, that is a clear signal to begin building organizational knowledge. Not by buying software, but by starting with the most critical processes and capturing the knowledge of how they are actually being executed.

---

## Conclusion — This isn't a technology problem

The organizational knowledge challenge in manufacturing isn't a technology problem. It's a problem of how an organization operates and learns.

Technology — from simple DMS to complex knowledge graphs, from digital SOPs to AI-assisted knowledge capture — can support each stage significantly. But technology only creates value when there is knowledge to manage and a culture of capturing it.

Organizations that begin building organizational knowledge today — even from the smallest first steps — will have a compounding advantage: operations less dependent on specific individuals, and a foundation that makes AI genuinely useful when deployed.

Organizations that continue leaving knowledge in people's heads will continue paying costs that are rarely calculated fully: onboarding costs, quality variation costs, inconsistent audit outcomes, and the cost of being unable to scale.
