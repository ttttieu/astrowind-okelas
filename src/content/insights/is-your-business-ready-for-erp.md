Most leaders begin the ERP conversation with one question: *"Which system should we go with?"*

That is not the wrong question. But it comes far too early.

The question that needs to come first — and is almost always skipped — is: **is your organization actually ready for ERP to deliver value?**

Not ready in terms of budget. Not ready in terms of intent. Ready in terms of the organizational conditions that determine whether an ERP project succeeds or quietly fails: processes, data, people, and governance.

---

## Why ERP Readiness Matters More Than ERP Selection

ERP is a process-driven system. It records transactions, connects data across departments, and generates reporting — but only when the organization has processes clear enough to configure, data clean enough to trust, and people willing to change how they work.

When those conditions are not in place, ERP does not create order. It encodes and amplifies the disorder that already exists — at significantly greater cost and complexity.

This is the reason many ERP projects end with software running but operations still driven by spreadsheets, email chains, and institutional knowledge held by a handful of people — exactly as before.

---

## 8 Questions to Assess Your Readiness

No consultant required. Just honest answers.

**1. Are your processes documented and consistently followed?**

If you ask three different employees how they handle the same task — say, the goods receiving process or a purchase approval — do you get the same answer? If not, ERP will run into difficulty at the configuration stage before the project has properly started.

**2. Is there someone with the authority and time to make decisions throughout the project?**

ERP implementation requires constant decisions: how should this process work, how should that exception be handled, should this new requirement be accepted. If there is no one with sufficient authority and available time to answer these questions throughout the project, progress will stall.

**3. What is the actual condition of your master data?**

Product catalog, vendor list, customer records, bill of materials — are these complete, current, and free of duplicates? Poor-quality data entered into ERP produces reports that no one trusts and no one uses.

**4. Does your inventory on paper reflect physical reality?**

This is particularly important for manufacturing businesses. If your recorded inventory regularly diverges from what is actually on the floor, that problem needs to be addressed before go-live, not after.

**5. Have you defined the initial implementation scope?**

Which modules go in first, which are deferred? Which entities or locations are in scope for phase one? An undefined scope will expand throughout implementation — pulling along additional cost, time, and risk.

**6. Do you have a change management plan?**

ERP changes how employees do their daily work. If your plan consists only of software training without a plan to help people understand *why* the change is happening and *what is in it for them*, resistance will be high and adoption will be slow.

**7. Who will own the system after the project ends?**

The implementation partner will leave. The question is: who is the internal ERP owner afterward? Who handles change requests? Who is accountable for data quality? If these are not answered before go-live, they become urgent problems shortly after.

**8. Is leadership committed to active participation — not just budget approval?**

This means being present for key decisions, attending project reviews, and sending a clear signal to the organization that this initiative is a priority. ERP implementations that are treated as IT projects — with leadership involvement limited to signing off on the budget — rarely achieve what they set out to do.

---

## If Your Honest Answer Is "Not Yet"

That is a useful answer, not a discouraging one.

It does not mean ERP is the wrong direction. It means the organization needs preparation first — and preparation done well typically shortens the actual implementation, reduces cost overruns, and substantially increases the likelihood that ERP generates lasting value after go-live.

The most common preparation steps:

- Document and standardize core operational processes before beginning.
- Clean master data before migration, not during.
- Define an initial scope small enough to succeed.
- Assign clear internal ownership for the project and for the system post go-live.
- Communicate clearly with employees about what is changing and why — before the project starts.
