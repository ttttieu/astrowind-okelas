> **Executive Summary**
>
> - Traditional eQMS is often framed as a large system requiring full deployment — which creates significant cost, time, and risk barriers for SMEs.
> - "Progressive eQMS" is a different approach: deploy the smallest unit with clear value that fits the organization's current foundation, operate and learn from it, then expand incrementally. This is not a compromise — it's the right strategy.
> - Starting small isn't a lesser version of the goal. It reduces implementation risk, builds organizational capability in parallel, and generates measurable value earlier.
> - "Progressive eQMS" is OKELAS's term for this approach — not a standardized industry term. The underlying principle is lean and incremental implementation applied to QMS.

## The problem with "implement everything at once"

When a CEO or Quality Director first explores eQMS, they often encounter a picture like this: a large system with dozens of modules — document control, CAPA, audit management, training management, supplier management, risk management, equipment management — all requiring deployment, user training, and historical data migration.

Attached to that picture is a significant budget and a long timeline.

For large organizations with dedicated IT and QA teams and appropriate budgets, this approach may be reasonable. But for manufacturing SMEs — which typically have 1–3 QA staff and constrained resources — this is an unnecessary barrier, and it typically leads to one of two poor outcomes:

**Outcome 1 — Nothing gets deployed.** Cost and complexity seem too large; the project gets indefinitely delayed while the problems remain.

**Outcome 2 — Deployment without utilization.** The full system gets purchased, installed, and trained on — but only 20–30% of features are actually used because the organization doesn't yet have the foundation (standardized processes, clean data, sufficiently proficient users) to use the rest. The unused portion becomes waste.

Neither outcome addresses the underlying problem.

---

## What progressive eQMS is

Progressive eQMS is not a specific type of software. It's an implementation approach: start with the smallest unit that has clear value and fits the organization's current foundation, operate and learn from it, then expand deliberately.

The core principle: **the next step should be defined by the actual problem being experienced and the real value it will create — not by a software vendor's feature list.**

In practice, this means instead of asking "which modules do we need?" — the question becomes:

*"What is the most important problem in our current quality management system — and what is the smallest portion of eQMS that could start solving it?"*

---

## Why starting small is the right strategy, not a compromise

Three important reasons why a progressive approach is often better than a full deployment — even when the organization has the resources for a full deployment.

### Reason 1 — Reduces real implementation risk

Implementing an eQMS is a significant change for users: workflows change, new systems need to be learned, and old habits need to be replaced. Large-scale change across many areas simultaneously creates high risk: if something goes wrong, the cause is hard to identify; if users don't adopt, the entire system is affected.

Progressive deployment reduces risk: problems are discovered and resolved in a small scope before expanding; users have time to adapt in stages; and if one part doesn't perform as expected, the impact is contained.

### Reason 2 — Builds organizational capability in parallel

An eQMS isn't only software — it requires changes in how processes are executed and records are captured. Those changes require time for users to genuinely become proficient.

When deploying everything at once, the organization typically can't build capability across all areas simultaneously. The result: some parts work well, others get neglected.

Stage-by-stage deployment allows capability to be built in one area before expanding to the next — ensuring each deployed portion is operating correctly before adding the next.

### Reason 3 — Creates value earlier and measurably

When a full deployment takes 12–18 months to go live, the organization waits a long time before seeing real value. And when it finally does go live, it's difficult to attribute which value came from which part of the system.

Deploying in smaller pieces creates value faster — in weeks or months rather than a year or more. And each piece can be evaluated on its own: did this portion solve the original problem? Is it worth expanding?

## Which modules to prioritize when starting

There's no single correct sequence for every organization — the starting point depends on the specific problem being experienced and the existing foundation. Several common patterns:

### Common starting point 1 — Document control

**When it fits:** The organization is experiencing problems with scattered documentation, uncontrolled versions, or audit findings related to document control.

**Immediate value:** Employees always see the currently in-effect version. Audit preparation becomes shorter because version control can be demonstrated.

**Foundation required:** At minimum, several core processes already documented in writing.

### Common starting point 2 — Non-conformance and CAPA

**When it fits:** The organization is experiencing recurring problems with no system to track root causes and corrective actions.

**Immediate value:** Can answer "has this problem been addressed and was the corrective action effective?" — from the system, not from memory.

**Foundation required:** A process for recording non-conformances needs to be established (even a simple one).

### Common starting point 3 — Training management

**When it fits:** Audit findings related to training, or high turnover requiring assurance that new employees are trained and documented.

**Immediate value:** Can demonstrate who was trained on what and when — in seconds rather than searching through paper files.

**Foundation required:** A list of required training for each role.

## A sample roadmap — from starting point to a more complete system

An illustrative progression for an 80-person food manufacturer maintaining ISO 22000:

*Note: This is a composite illustrative scenario. An actual roadmap depends on the specific problems and foundation of each organization.*

**Months 1–3: Document control for core quality documents**

Deploy version control and approval workflow for 15–20 most important documents: incoming inspection SOP, production control SOP, finished goods inspection SOP, and several quality policies.

Goal: audits can demonstrate document control for this set of documents.

**Months 4–6: Non-conformance tracking**

Add module for recording non-conformances and tracking corrective actions. Link to document control: when a non-conformance involves a document, link directly.

Goal: can answer "has this problem occurred before?" and "was the previous corrective action effective?"

**Months 7–12: Expand document control + basic audit management**

Expand document control to remaining documents. Add audit scheduling and checklist management so internal audits can be executed and recorded in the system.

Goal: audit preparation significantly reduced because most records are captured during normal operations.

**Year 2+: Supplier management, equipment calibration, training — in order of priority based on actual problems**

Each module added when there's a clear need and the foundation is ready — not according to a vendor's feature checklist.

## When to consider progressive eQMS

Progressive eQMS is most appropriate for:

- Manufacturing SMEs with 1–3 QA staff and moderate budgets — insufficient resources for full simultaneous deployment.
- Organizations maintaining ISO but with specific compliance gaps to address as priorities.
- Organizations with recurring audit findings in specific areas — progressive approach allows focusing on those areas first.
- Businesses wanting to validate value before committing to larger investment.

Progressive eQMS is less suitable (though the principles still apply) for:

- Organizations building a system from scratch with a specific certification deadline — a clearer timeline and potentially faster pace may be needed.
- Pharmaceutical GMP environments with complex validation requirements — validation requirements may demand a certain level of completeness.

---

## Conclusion

The reason SMEs often don't implement eQMS isn't usually that they don't recognize the need — it's that they understand eQMS as a large, expensive, complex system that can only be deployed in full.

Progressive eQMS is a different lens: start from the actual problem being experienced, find the smallest portion that can address it, deploy and learn from that, then expand deliberately. This is how to reduce risk, generate value earlier, and build a system that actually fits the organization's real needs.

Not every feature in a software catalogue is needed from day one. But starting in the right place, at the right time, is both the hardest and the most important step.
