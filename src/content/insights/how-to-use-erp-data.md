## What Data Is Your ERP Already Generating?

Every day, in an organization with a functioning ERP, the system records hundreds to thousands of events:

- Every purchase order created, approved, received, and paid.
- Every material received into inventory, issued to production, and finished goods returned to stock.
- Every production order created, started, and completed — with actual quantities, actual times, and actual yield loss.
- Every sales order, delivery, invoice, and payment.
- Every corresponding accounting entry across all of the above.

Taken together, this is a valuable operational data asset — if used correctly. It can answer questions like: which products are actually most profitable after full cost allocation? Which suppliers have the highest on-time delivery rate? Which production operations consistently exceed standard time? Which inventory is turning slowly and incurring holding cost?

But first, a practical question needs answering: *why do most organizations not yet extract this kind of insight from ERP?*

---

## Why ERP Data Goes Unused

Three categories of causes are most common:

### Data quality is insufficient for analysis

This is the most common barrier and the least acknowledged. ERP data is entered by many people, in different ways, at different times. Without rigorous data management processes, the result is:

- The same product categorized differently across time periods.
- Costs recorded inconsistently across accounts.
- Production actuals entered in daily batches rather than in real time, losing time-based accuracy.

When data is inconsistent, any analysis derived from it is unreliable — and users know it. This is why many ERP reports are generated but not actually used.

### The right questions have not been defined

ERP can generate a very large number of reports — but it does not know which reports matter to this business, at this moment, for this decision-maker.

Many organizations have not answered the foundational question: *"What do we want to know — and what decisions will we use that information to make?"* Without an answer to this question, ERP data exists as a storage archive — not as a source of insight.

### Missing context to interpret the data

This is the subtlest cause. ERP data records *what* happened — not *why* it happened.

Why did last month's production batch have higher-than-normal yield loss? Why has on-time payment from a customer segment declined? Why did a supplier start delivering late last quarter?

Answering these questions requires ERP data combined with *context* — knowledge about the market, about operational processes, about events that occurred during the period. That context lives in people's heads — not in ERP. And when those people leave, the context goes with them.

## Four Levels of ERP Data Utilization

A practical framework for understanding where an organization is and where it could go:

### Level 1 — Transaction recording

ERP is used to enter and store transactions. Reporting is primarily list-based: lists of orders, lists of unpaid invoices, inventory stock lists. Data exists but has not been processed into information.

*Most manufacturing SMEs are here.*

### Level 2 — Operational reporting

Data is aggregated into periodic reports: monthly revenue, inventory by category, costs by department. Reports show *what* is happening, but primarily look backward and lack analytical depth.

*Many organizations reach this level within 12–18 months of ERP go-live.*

### Level 3 — Analysis and insight

ERP data is combined and analyzed across multiple dimensions: profitability by product and channel, supplier performance across multi-dimensional criteria, yield loss trends over time and by machine. The question is no longer "how much" but "why" and "compared to what."

*This is the level at which ERP begins to generate real competitive advantage.*

### Level 4 — Forecasting and decision support

Historical data is used to forecast demand, optimize inventory, and detect anomalies early before they become significant problems. AI and machine learning can contribute at this level — but only when data from levels 1–3 is of sufficient quality and historical depth.

*This level requires data infrastructure and analytical capability that most SMEs have not yet developed.*

## What Is Actually Needed to Move Higher

It is not simply a matter of adding BI tools or dashboards. Moving to a higher level of ERP data utilization requires:

**Data that is clean and consistent.** This is a non-negotiable prerequisite. No analytical tool can generate trustworthy insight from poor-quality data. Invest in data governance before investing in BI.

**Business questions that are clearly defined.** Start from decisions — *"Which business decisions do we need to make regularly where we currently lack the data to decide well?"* — then identify the data and reporting required to support them. Not the other way around.

**Context for interpretation.** ERP data needs to be combined with knowledge about operational processes, the market, and what is actually happening in the organization. This requires not just technical tools but a way of organizing knowledge — so that context is not only in people's heads but is captured, stored, and connected to data.

**ERP knowledge — understanding the structure and logic of the data.** ERP data is not always intuitive. To analyze it correctly, one must understand how it is structured, what fields mean, and how modules connect to each other. This knowledge is typically lost when the implementation partner leaves — and reconstructing it is one of the least recognized challenges of the post-go-live phase.

---

## From ERP Data to Organizational Intelligence

When ERP data is used correctly, it is not just a source of reports — it becomes a component of organizational intelligence.

Organizational intelligence is not simply data. It is the organization's capacity to understand what is happening, explain why it is happening, and make decisions based on evidence rather than intuition.

ERP creates the data foundation for this — but moving from data to intelligence requires two additional layers: *context* (understanding why data looks the way it does) and *organizational knowledge* (knowledge about processes, about people, about how the business actually operates).

This is the gap that many organizations are trying to fill with AI — but AI can only operate effectively when it has sufficient context and data quality to work with. Without those, AI has data but not understanding.
