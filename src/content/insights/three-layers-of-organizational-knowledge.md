> **Executive Summary**
>
> - A shared document repository is not knowledge management. Company knowledge comes in three kinds: knowledge that arrives from outside, knowledge the company sets for itself, and knowledge generated by daily work. Each needs different controls.
> - The three layers are External Knowledge (what is required of us), Internal Knowledge (how we decided to do the work) and Operational Knowledge (what actually happened). The third is the one most often missed, and it is the one that produces evidence.
> - The value sits in the joins between layers: requirement → internal rule → actual record. When the layers connect, you can trace in both directions.
> - You do not need to start with software. Start by finding out what knowledge you have, where it lives and who owns it, then pick the smallest system that solves the problem.

Picture a familiar, illustrative situation. An employee finds three versions of the same SOP in three different folders. One was formally issued. One was edited but never approved. One is the version people are actually using on the shop floor. The repository is not short of anything, yet nobody is sure which version to follow.

If you are starting [organizational knowledge management](/en/insights/knowledge-management/knowledge-management-manufacturing), this article helps you avoid a common mistake: pouring every kind of knowledge into one store and calling it done. You will get a simple way to classify knowledge, a set of self-check questions, and a staged way to begin.

## Why one shared repository is not enough

When companies think of knowledge management (KM), they often picture a central document repository, digitized SOPs, stored forms or an AI chatbot for staff to query. Those are useful steps. They are not sufficient.

A real company runs on several sources of knowledge: external regulations, industry standards, internal procedures, and the information generated every day in production, sales and management. If all of it goes into one store without regard to what kind it is, these problems appear quickly:

- Staff use regulations or standards that are no longer in force.
- SOPs are not updated when legal requirements or actual practice change.
- Departments work from different versions of the same form.
- Incident-handling experience lives only in a few people's memory.
- Leadership cannot reconstruct the basis and evidence for a decision.

The root cause is the same in each case. Different kinds of knowledge differ in three ways: who has authority over the content, how long it stays valid, and how you prove it is being applied. A regulator's circular, an SOP your company approved, and a quality-check record written at 2 p.m. yesterday cannot be managed under one rule.

## Three layers: external, internal and operational

This split has a basis outside OKELAS. ISO 9001:2015, clause 7.1.6, requires an organization to determine the knowledge needed to operate its processes, and its notes name two groups of sources: internal (for example experience, lessons from successes and failures, and undocumented knowledge) and external (for example standards, academia, conferences, customers and external providers).

OKELAS takes one extra step. Internal knowledge mixes what the company prescribes (SOPs, policies) with what actually gets recorded (records, incidents, decisions). Those two have different lifecycles and different traceability needs, so we separate them into two layers. This is OKELAS analysis, not an industry-standard term.

| Layer | Core question | Who decides the content | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| External | What is required of us? | Regulators, standards bodies, customers, the market | Sets the basis and the limits |
| Internal | How have we decided to do it? | The company, through an approval process | Sets the method and the responsibilities |
| Operational | What actually happened? | Daily operations, captured by systems and people | Records performance and evidence |

![Three cards side by side: external knowledge answers what is required of us, internal knowledge answers how we decided to do it, operational knowledge answers what actually happened.](~/assets/images/insights/three-layers-of-organizational-knowledge/tlk-01-three-layers-en-dark.svg)
*Figure 1. The three layers and the core question each one answers.*

## Layer 1 — External Knowledge: what comes from outside

This is knowledge that originates outside the company but directly or indirectly shapes its operations:

- **Regulatory:** laws, decrees, circulars, regulator rules and compliance requirements.
- **Industry Standards:** ISO, GMP, HACCP, GxP and sector-specific standards.
- **Guidelines:** technical guidance and good-practice documents from competent bodies.
- **Customer Requirements:** technical specifications, quality standards and contract terms.
- **Market & Technology:** market trends, new technology and changes that may affect strategy.

Storing these documents is not the point. The company needs to answer four questions:

1. Where did the document come from, and is the source reliable?
2. Is it in force, or has it been replaced?
3. Which activity, product or department does it apply to?
4. When an external requirement changes, which internal processes must be reviewed?

This layer matters most in pharmaceuticals, food, medical devices and GMP manufacturing. Requirements change outside your control, but the burden of showing you have met them stays with you.

## Layer 2 — Internal Knowledge: what the company has decided

If External Knowledge answers what the company must comply with, Internal Knowledge answers how the company chose to organize and perform the work. It covers knowledge created, approved or prescribed inside the organization:

- **SOP & Work Instructions:** standard operating procedures and task instructions.
- **Policies & Regulations:** policies, rules and internal regulations.
- **Templates & Forms:** forms, document templates, report structures and record-keeping guidance.
- **Organizational Knowledge:** structure, functions, authority and responsibilities.
- **Technical & Professional Knowledge:** technical know-how, working methods and expert guidance.
- **Lessons Learned:** what projects, incidents and improvements taught, once reviewed and built into rules or training.

The three-SOP situation above is an example. The problem is no longer finding a document. It is information control: which version is in force, who approved it, and why the shop floor is using another.

One principle to hold on to: internal documents should not be standalone files. An SOP should be linked to the responsible department, the people who perform it, the related forms and the process it governs. That is also the line between [a DMS and knowledge management](/en/insights/knowledge-management/dms-vs-knowledge-management): a DMS manages documents, while KM manages what the documents mean in your operating context. When SOPs exist but are not followed, the cause often sits right here, as we discuss in [why SOPs are not followed](/en/insights/knowledge-management/why-sops-are-not-followed).

## Layer 3 — Operational Knowledge: what actual work produces

This is the layer most often missed when companies start KM. External Knowledge tells you what is required. Internal Knowledge sets how you organize the work. Operational Knowledge shows what is really going on. It forms continuously:

- **Transaction Data:** orders, transactions, production, purchasing and sales.
- **Workflow Records:** who did the work, its status, who approved it, when it finished.
- **Decision Records:** management decisions, their basis and who is accountable.
- **Quality & Compliance Records:** inspection results, deviations, CAPA, minutes and compliance evidence.
- **Operational Experience:** incidents, abnormal situations, how they were handled and what was learned.
- **Performance Data:** operating metrics, productivity, quality and cost.

For example, an SOP says product must pass a quality check before release. That document belongs to the Internal layer. But you also need to know which batch was checked, who did it, what the result was, who handled any deviation, who had authority to allow work to continue or stop it, and what evidence confirms the whole procedure was carried out. Those answers live in the Operational layer. A repository of SOPs or a document Q&A chatbot cannot supply them.

In GMP settings the record requirements are explicit. WHO guidance on data and record management (TRS 996, Annex 5, 2016) sets out the ALCOA principles: data should be attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original and accurate. That text was later replaced by the data integrity guideline in TRS 1033, Annex 4 (2021). In short, operational records are not reference material. They are evidence.

This layer also reduces dependence on individual memory. When someone leaves or a manager is absent, the organization can still retrieve the work history, the reasoning behind handling decisions and the decisions that were made. It is the manageable form of the problem described in [what key employees take with them when they leave](/en/insights/knowledge-management/knowledge-loss-when-employees-leave).

## The three layers must be connected

The three layers are not three separate data stores. KM earns its value in the relationships. Take an illustrative manufacturing example:

- **External:** a standard or regulation requires control of a production step.
- **Internal:** an SOP sets the control method, the check frequency and the person responsible.
- **Operational:** records capture the check result, who did it, when, any deviation and the corrective action.

Once the layers are connected, two things become possible:

- **Forward tracing.** When a new regulation arrives, you can identify which SOPs are affected. When an SOP changes, you can identify the departments and people involved.
- **Backward tracing.** When a deviation occurs, you can retrieve the relevant requirement, the procedure and the actual evidence.

![Three connected boxes in order: external requirement, internal SOP, operational record. One row of arrows runs forward showing that a changed requirement means reviewing SOPs and people; another runs backward showing that a deviation traces back to the SOP and its basis.](~/assets/images/insights/three-layers-of-organizational-knowledge/tlk-02-trace-chain-en-dark.svg)
*Figure 2. Illustrative example: forward and backward tracing across the three layers.*

The connection also runs in a loop. A lesson from the Operational layer, once reviewed and approved, goes back to revise an SOP in the Internal layer. And if practice shows an external requirement is not being fully met, it becomes input for reviewing the process itself. This is the chain OKELAS builds its operating model on: Process → Workflow → Event → Evidence → Knowledge → Decision → Action.

### Where the boundaries blur

In practice, many items sit near a boundary. A simple test: ask who decides the content, and where it came from.

| Item | Layer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Technical requirement in a customer contract | External | The customer set it. Once you turn it into an SOP, that SOP is Internal |
| Lesson from an incident, approved and built into an SOP or training | Internal | It has become an organizational rule |
| Record of the incident and how it was handled at the time | Operational | Evidence of what actually happened |
| A blank form | Internal | Designed and approved by the company |
| A completed form | Operational | A record of execution |
| A management decision | Operational | It is a record; its basis points back to Internal or External |

One signal deserves attention: when shop-floor practice differs from the approved SOP, the gap between the Internal and Operational layers is the first place to look.

## Self-check: six questions across three layers

Each layer has two questions. Answer "yes", "no" or "not sure".

![Three cards with two self-check questions each: external asks about tracking what is in force and tracing affected SOPs; internal asks about a single version in force and linking SOPs to people, forms and processes; operational asks about retrieving inspection records and the basis of decisions.](~/assets/images/insights/three-layers-of-organizational-knowledge/tlk-03-six-checks-en-dark.svg)
*Figure 3. Six self-check questions. A discussion aid, not a certified methodology.*

If you answer "no" or "not sure" to three or more, the problem is probably not your search tool or chatbot. It is how your knowledge is organized. The sensible next step is a structured assessment, not more software.

## Which tools to start with

Not every company needs a dedicated knowledge platform right away. The choice should follow your size, complexity and governance needs. The table below shows a workable starting point for each layer, and the signal that you need something more structured.

| Layer | You can start with | You need a structured system when |
|---|---|---|
| External | An AI workspace (for example Claude Projects) over a curated set of legal, standards and guidance documents, for Q&A, summaries and version comparison. Or linked notes (for example Obsidian) connecting regulations, clauses and topics. Always check source, validity and basis; do not treat AI output as final legal confirmation | You need systematic control of sources, versions, validity, scope of application, compliance obligations and the link between external requirements and internal processes |
| Internal | A separate Project per department or function, loaded with SOPs and policies as context for lookup and drafting. Control access, and keep sensitive data out of environments that have not been approved. Linked notes can serve a small team | You need document lifecycle control, permissions, approvals, revision history, and links between SOPs, forms and processes, especially for ISO, GMP or other quality systems |
| Operational | An AI workspace to analyze extracted, curated operating datasets, spot trends and support root-cause investigation. Linked notes for experts to keep lessons learned and project logs | You need to record transactions, approvals and records that must be traceable. That is the job of ERP, QMS and workflow systems, not of AI or note-taking tools |

A practical path has three stages:

1. **Identify and classify.** Find out which knowledge sources the company owns, where they sit, who is responsible and what problems are already showing up.
2. **Standardize and connect.** Set up the knowledge structure, document control rules, access rights, update responsibilities and the links between the three layers.
3. **Integrate into operations.** Connect knowledge to workflow, ERP, QMS and business systems. Bring AI in step by step for search, explanation, analysis and decision support, grounded in data and evidence you can trace.

![Three steps rising left to right: stage one identify and classify, stage two standardize and connect, stage three integrate into operations, each with the type of tooling that usually fits.](~/assets/images/insights/three-layers-of-organizational-knowledge/tlk-04-three-stages-en-dark.svg)
*Figure 4. Three stages for starting knowledge governance. A discussion approach, not an industry standard.*

The question not to start with is "which KM software should we buy?" Ask first: how well do we manage knowledge today, and which gaps create risk or slow us down? For an SME, the principle to keep is to solve the operating problem with the smallest sufficient system, not to build a large one to prove the technology.

## How OKELAS approaches the three layers

This section is OKELAS's viewpoint and approach, not a measured result.

OKELAS does not treat the three layers as three separate stores. [Knowledge is infrastructure](/en/why/knowledge-as-infrastructure), and infrastructure only has value when its parts connect. In practice:

- **Connect instead of separate.** OKELAS Core manages the relationships among people, processes, documents, events, evidence and knowledge, so an SOP is no longer a lone file but is tied to its owner, its forms and its execution records.
- **ERP is the transaction backbone, not the whole answer.** For suitable companies, ERPNext can serve as the system of record for transactions and operating data. The ERP Knowledge Graph helps make sense of ERP structure, workflow and customizations, so the Operational layer holds context as well as data. ERPNext can exist independently of OKELAS.
- **AI answers inside organizational context.** A copilot or agent is only useful when it answers through explanation, evidence and trace (Explain → Evidence → Trace), not just a plausible-sounding reply. That is why the layers need to be connected before AI is added, as in the [Knowledge & Copilot](/en/solutions/knowledge-copilot) approach.
- **Go step by step.** A company can begin by standardizing documents and processes, then expand along the [progressive eQMS](/en/solutions/progressive-eqms) path. Where data is sensitive or compliance demands it, on-premise deployment is an option worth weighing.

## Conclusion

A company can own thousands of documents and still lack the capability to manage knowledge. It can run ERP, DMS, QMS and several AI tools and still struggle to connect information across departments. The real problem is not too few documents or too little technology. It is that three kinds of knowledge are being treated as one.

Separate them so each is managed for what it is, then connect them so you can trace from requirement to evidence. That is what makes knowledge part of how the business operates, rather than something that is merely stored.
