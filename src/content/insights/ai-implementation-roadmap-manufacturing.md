> **Executive Summary**
>
> - AI hype pushes SMEs toward two counterproductive responses: deploying quickly without a foundation, or waiting indefinitely for AI to "mature." Both have real costs.
> - A realistic AI roadmap for manufacturing SMEs doesn't start with "which AI tool?" — it starts with assessing current state and identifying the next step with the clearest economic case.
> - Three practical stages: foundation building (process, data, context), supported individual + lookup AI, and AI within workflow. Each stage has entry conditions, measurable outcomes, and clear criteria for moving forward.
> - Not every organization needs to reach stage three. The goal is to reach the stage with the clearest economic value for that specific organization — not the most technologically advanced stage.

## The problem with AI hype

The 2023–2024 period saw an AI hype cycle with few recent parallels. Every conference, every business publication, every leadership conversation included AI.

For manufacturing SMEs, this hype creates real pressure — not from the market or customers, but from internal expectation: *"Other companies are doing AI. We need to do something."*

And "doing something" typically leads to one of two suboptimal outcomes.

**Outcome 1 — Deploy quickly without a foundation.**
Subscribe to AI tools, ask employees to use them, expect fast results. After 3–6 months, AI is being used as a high-speed drafting assistant — with no organizational impact. Money spent, results unclear.

**Outcome 2 — Wait indefinitely.**
"AI is changing too fast. Let's wait until it stabilizes." Meanwhile, competitors are accumulating experience, building foundations, and learning from real deployments — while this organization remains on the sidelines.

Both carry real costs. The question isn't "should we do AI?" — it's "where do we start and in what sequence, so each step provides the foundation for the next."

---

## The real starting point — not selecting an AI tool

Most AI discussions begin with the wrong question: *"Which AI tool is right for us?"*

The right prior question: *"Where are we now, and what is the next step with the clearest economic justification?"*

Answering that question requires an honest assessment of current state across three critical dimensions.

**Dimension 1 — Process.**
How many important operational processes are documented in writing? At what level of detail? AI cannot execute an undefined process — it can only accelerate one that already exists.

**Dimension 2 — Data.**
Is critical operational data (quality inspection, batch records, production) stored in a consistently structured, queryable format? Or is it distributed across inconsistent Excel files and paper records?

**Dimension 3 — Knowledge and context.**
Who is currently holding critical knowledge about how the business operates? If 2–3 of those people left, could the organization continue operating normally? Is that knowledge documented and linked to processes?

The results of this three-dimension assessment — not a features comparison of AI tools — is the genuine starting point.

## An illustrative scenario: 120-person food processing company

*Note: This is a composite scenario synthesized from characteristics common to small and mid-sized manufacturing businesses. It is not a case study of a specific organization. Figures are illustrative estimates.*

---

A food processing company, 120 employees, producing for export markets while maintaining ISO 22000 certification. Leadership wants to apply AI but doesn't know where to start.

**Initial assessment findings:**

- Process: approximately 60% of critical processes have written SOPs; 40% depend on shift supervisor experience and verbal knowledge transfer.
- Data: quality inspection results are recorded in Excel, but with different formats across three QC staff members. Production data from the ERP system is not connected to QC data.
- Knowledge: two core shift supervisors hold significant knowledge about handling production line anomalies — none of this is documented.
- Staff have begun using personal ChatGPT accounts but with no policy or guidance.

**What can be done immediately — and what needs to come first:**

Rather than adding another AI tool, leadership decides to proceed in this sequence:

*Months 1–3: Foundation*
Standardize QC data recording into a single consistent schema. Complete documentation for the remaining 40% of undocumented processes — not all at once, but prioritizing the five highest-risk processes. Capture the shift supervisors' anomaly-handling knowledge into structured written documentation.

*Months 4–6: Supported AI*
Deploy an internal chatbot based on the now-standardized SOP documentation. Employees can look up procedures faster. QA uses AI to draft inspection reports. AI is not involved in any decisions.

*Months 7–12: Evaluate and selectively expand*
After 6 months with the chatbot: review which questions it handles well and which it fails. Use those findings to determine: which additional data needs to be connected, which processes need further standardization, and which specific workflow step might be suitable for agent support.

**Observed results after 9 months (illustrative):**
- New employee onboarding time reduced meaningfully because process documentation is now standardized.
- Audit preparation shorter because QC records now have consistent structure.
- The AI chatbot is being used for actual work — procedure lookup and report drafting — not just as a meeting minutes generator.
- Leadership has a clearer picture of what the next investment should be.

Most importantly: this organization didn't start with "which AI tool?" — they started with "where are we, and what is the next step with a clear basis?"

## A three-stage AI adoption framework for manufacturing SMEs

From the illustrative scenario and practical analysis, a three-stage framework can be mapped that applies across most manufacturing SMEs — with entry conditions, required outcomes, and criteria for progressing.

---

### Stage 1 — Foundation

**Objective:** Build the organizational foundation that allows AI to function meaningfully.

**Core work:**
- Standardize critical operational data into a consistent, queryable schema.
- Document critical processes that currently lack written SOPs, prioritized by risk level.
- Identify and capture knowledge currently residing in specific individuals — before those people leave.
- Establish basic AI governance: who can use which tools, within what scope.

**AI at this stage:** Personal productivity tools only — AI supports drafting, summarizing, translating. AI is not involved in decisions or processes. Staff learn to work with AI in a bounded, lower-risk context.

**Required outcomes before advancing:** At minimum 80% of critical processes documented to the level a new employee could execute them. QC and production data have a consistent schema. A basic AI policy for staff exists.

**Reference timeline:** 3–6 months depending on starting point and organizational size.

---

### Stage 2 — Supported AI

**Objective:** Deploy AI into applications with clear value, low risk, and measurable outcomes.

**Core work:**
- Deploy an internal chatbot based on standardized SOPs and documentation.
- Identify 2–3 high-value, lower-risk use cases — procedure lookup, report drafting, new employee onboarding support.
- Measure: task time before and after, output quality, actual usage rates.
- Document: which questions the chatbot handles well and which it fails — to guide stage 3 investment decisions.

**AI at this stage:** Internal chatbot for lookup and task support. Individual AI productivity tools deployed with clearer guidance. No agents taking autonomous action in processes.

**Required outcomes before advancing:** Chatbot in real use, not just pilot. Data available on what the chatbot handles well and where it fails. Leadership has clear visibility into next-stage gaps.

**Reference timeline:** 3–6 months of real operation.

---

### Stage 3 — AI within workflow

**Objective:** Integrate AI into operational workflows at specific steps where conditions and value are clear.

**Entry conditions (required):**
- Operational data from at least one critical system (ERP, QMS) is structured well enough for AI to query and analyze.
- At least one process is defined in sufficient detail for an agent to participate in a specific step.
- Agent governance is defined: what the agent is authorized to do, who reviews its output, how the audit trail works.

**Core work:**
- Identify 1–2 specific workflows for agent participation — don't deploy broadly yet.
- Build agents with clear scope, human review mechanisms, and audit trails.
- Measure organizational impact — not only individual task speed.

**AI at this stage:** Agents participating in specific workflows. Organizational intelligence begins to be built — not only AI productivity for individuals.

**Important note:** Not every organization needs to reach stage 3. For many manufacturing SMEs, a well-operated stage 2 delivers sufficient value to justify the investment. Stage 3 fits best when stage 2 has revealed specific, high-value gaps and opportunities.

## The one thing worth remembering

AI hype will continue. Tools will keep changing. The model available tomorrow will be better than today's.

But the organizational foundation — defined processes, structured data, captured knowledge — doesn't become outdated the same way. Investment in foundation is investment that accumulates value regardless of which direction AI technology evolves.

And organizations that build that foundation will be better positioned to take advantage of whatever AI generation comes next than organizations that are waiting for AI to become "good enough."

AI is already good enough for many genuinely useful applications today. The question is: is the organization ready to use it?
