> **Executive Summary**
>
> - "Where should we invest in digitalization next?" is a question whose answer depends entirely on where the business currently stands — there is no universally correct next step.
> - The right framework isn't "what is the ROI of this investment?" but rather "which problem is costing the most and what is the smallest step that could begin to address it?"
> - The next economically meaningful step is the one that satisfies all three conditions: solves a real problem, fits the current foundation, and creates measurable value.
> - This article provides a 4-question framework and a mapping table of next steps by current maturity level.

---

## Why "next digitalization investment" is a hard question

When a CEO or COO asks "where should we invest in digitalization next?", they typically receive one of two types of answers:

**Type 1 — From software vendors:** "Our product can help you achieve X, Y, Z with an X% ROI." This answer is clearly biased and doesn't start from the organization's actual problems.

**Type 2 — From general advisors:** "Companies need ERP first, then eQMS, then AI." This sequence may be generally correct but doesn't account for the specific situation, budget, and priorities of a particular business.

Neither type of answer starts from where the business actually stands.

---

## A necessary principle to establish first

![Two common answers about the next digitalization investment, from vendors and from general advisors, neither starting from where the business stands; and the principle for choosing the right step](/assets/images/insights/next-step-digitalization-roi/nx-01-two-answers-one-principle-en.svg)

Before the framework: **the right next step is not the most technologically advanced step — it's the step that solves the most specific problem at the most appropriate cost and complexity.**

This means:

- Sometimes the next step isn't new software but process standardization.
- Sometimes the next step is using existing software more effectively rather than buying more.
- Sometimes the next step is a simple DMS rather than a comprehensive ERP.
- And sometimes the next step really is a larger investment — when the foundation is ready and the problem is significant enough to justify it.

There is no single correct answer. There is the most appropriate answer for the organization's specific position.

## The 4-question self-assessment framework

![The four-question framework: the costliest problem, level and weakest dimension, conditions for success, and how value is measured over 6 to 12 months](/assets/images/insights/next-step-digitalization-roi/nx-02-four-questions-en.svg)

This isn't a ROI calculation formula. It's a set of questions for CEOs and leadership teams to work through before making an investment decision.

### Question 1 — Which problem is costing the most?

Don't start from "what do we want to achieve" — start from "what are we losing."

More specifically: look back at the past 12 months and identify the 3 operational problems that have cost the most time, money, or opportunity. These might include:

- Audit preparation taking several weeks
- Quality inconsistency across shifts
- Significant operational disruption when key employees are absent
- Slow decisions because information consolidation takes too long
- High error rates from manual processes

The problems identified here become the test for any investment under consideration: "does this investment address one of these problems?"

### Question 2 — Which stage is the business at — and which dimension is weakest?

As analyzed in the article on the 5 digitalization stages: not every department is at the same level. More importantly: not every investment delivers value at every stage.

Some investments don't create value without sufficient foundation — AI analytics is valueless if data isn't well-structured. And some investments needed at stage 2 become unnecessary if that stage has already been bypassed.

Specific question: "Which department or process is at the lowest stage — and what risk or cost is that creating?"

The next step usually should be: bring the weakest dimension up to the next stage, not upgrade an already-strong dimension further.

### Question 3 — What conditions are required for the next step to succeed — and are they in place?

The article on the big project trap analyzed this mechanism: many projects fail not because the technology is poor but because the foundation wasn't ready.

For any investment under consideration, identify the required foundation conditions:

- How standardized do processes need to be before this step is meaningful?
- What data structure is needed before this step can be utilized?
- What awareness and skills do users need before this step gets adopted?
- Who will own and develop the system after implementation?

If one or more conditions aren't in place — the question is: "can we and are we willing to create that condition?" If yes, continue. If not, this step may not be the right one right now.

### Question 4 — How will the value of this step be measured in the next 6–12 months?

This question forces specific value definition before investment — not after.

Not: "this system will make us more efficient." But: "in the next 12 months, which metric will change, by how much, and how will we measure it?"

Concrete examples:
- "Audit preparation time will decrease from 3 weeks to under 5 days"
- "Recurring non-conformance rate will decrease because we have a CAPA tracking system"
- "The CEO will be able to answer 5 key operational questions in 10 minutes instead of waiting 2–3 days"

If specific metrics can't be identified — that's a signal that the step isn't clear enough to invest in, or that expectations need to be clarified first.

## Mapping the next step to the current maturity level

![Reference table of the next step that usually makes the most economic sense for each digitalization level, from paper-based operations to AI-assisted operations](/assets/images/insights/next-step-digitalization-roi/nx-03-next-step-by-level-en.svg)

Based on the 4-question framework and the 5 digitalization stages, a reference mapping table can be sketched — not as a rigid rule but as a starting point for discussion.

| Current stage (weakest dimension) | Next step that typically has the highest economic value |
|---|---|
| Level 1 — Paper-based | Document the 5–10 most important processes. Begin capturing operational data in structured format (not PDF). |
| Level 2 — Scattered digital documents | DMS with version control for core quality documents. Standardize operational data capture format. |
| Level 3 — Basic workflow | eQMS module for a specific compliance problem (NC/CAPA or training management). Or: integrate data between existing systems. |
| Level 4 — Well-integrated | Knowledge structure so AI has context. Expand eQMS to remaining modules. |
| Level 5 — AI-supported | Expand AI to more workflows. Build organizational intelligence. |

*Note: This table is directional reference — not prescriptive. The right step depends on the specific problem, foundation, and resources of each organization.*

## What makes an investment step genuinely economically meaningful

![Three conditions for an economically meaningful digitalization step: solves a real problem, fits the foundation, creates measurable value; and the signal when each is missing](/assets/images/insights/next-step-digitalization-roi/nx-04-three-conditions-en.svg)

Synthesizing, the next economically meaningful step is one that simultaneously satisfies three conditions:

**1. Solves a real problem** — not investing in technology because it's "good" or "advanced," but because it addresses a specific problem that is costing the business real money.

**2. Fits the current foundation** — the foundation (processes, data, people) is sufficient for the step to work meaningfully. Not investing in a step whose prerequisites aren't in place.

**3. Creates measurable value** — it's possible to specify what value this step will create in 6–12 months and how it will be measured.

If a proposed investment doesn't satisfy all three conditions, that's a signal to reconsider — either the problem isn't clear enough, the foundation isn't ready, or the value hasn't been defined specifically enough.

## From framework to decision

This framework doesn't produce a single answer. It creates a structured conversation — and from that conversation, the most appropriate next step usually becomes significantly clearer.

If after working through the 4 questions the answer is still unclear — that's often a signal that a more thorough assessment of the organization's current state is needed before making an investment decision.
