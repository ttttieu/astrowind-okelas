> **Executive Summary**
>
> - Many industry reports cite high failure rates for large digital transformation projects — but these figures vary considerably depending on how "failure" is defined and the scope of each study. This article doesn't use those figures as evidence.
> - More useful is understanding why large projects tend to fail — because understanding the mechanism is what makes it possible to design around the risk, not just knowing the rate.
> - Three common failure mechanisms: scope exceeding the organization's capacity to absorb change, organizations not ready when projects start, and short-term result expectations from long-term change.
> - Not every large project fails — and not every failure is avoidable. But many failures can be prevented by choosing the right scope and approach from the start.

## The problem with "X% of digital transformation projects fail"

Before getting into the analysis, it's worth addressing a type of statistic that commonly appears on this topic.

You may have encountered figures like "70–85% of digital transformation projects fail" from various sources. These numbers sound compelling and are easy to cite — but they have significant methodological problems: the definition of "failure" varies considerably between studies, sample sizes range from a few hundred to several thousand companies across many different industries and geographies, and "digital transformation" itself is defined very differently.

This article doesn't use those figures as evidence.

Instead, it focuses on something more practically valuable: analyzing the mechanisms by which large projects tend to fail — because only by understanding the mechanism can projects be designed to avoid it.

---

## Why "big bang" projects carry disproportionate risk

Not every large project fails. But certain characteristics of the "big bang" approach — implementing everything at once — create structurally higher risk compared to a phased approach.

### Structural risk 1 — Problems discovered too late

In a big bang project, real-world results are only visible when the complete system goes live — typically after 12–24 months of implementation. If there are problems with the design, with initial assumptions, or with how users actually interact with the system — those problems are discovered when it's too late to adjust without discarding work already invested.

In a phased project, problems are discovered early — in a small scope, before the impact spreads.

### Structural risk 2 — Too much change at once

Digital transformation isn't only about installing software — it's changing how people work. Large-scale change across many areas simultaneously places very high demands on change management — and this is consistently the most underestimated element of large projects.

When too many things change simultaneously, users become overwhelmed, adoption rates drop, and large portions of the system get neglected. Result: significant investment, but only a fraction of features actually used.

### Structural risk 3 — Budget and timeline overruns

Large projects tend to finish later and cost more than planned — this is a broadly observed pattern across many types of complex projects, not only digitalization. And when budget and timeline overrun, the pressure to "show results immediately" increases — leading to rushed decisions to demonstrate ROI rather than allowing the project to be implemented correctly.

## Three most common traps

### Trap 1 — Scope defined by ambition, not by organizational capacity

This is the most common trap and also the most dangerous.

When a CEO or BOD decides "we're going to digitalize all operations," scope is defined by the desired outcome — not by a realistic assessment of the organization's capacity to implement and absorb the change.

The problem: every organization's capacity to absorb change has limits. Not every department is ready to change simultaneously. Not every process can be digitalized on the same timeline.

When scope exceeds absorption capacity, some parts get neglected — usually the hardest or most important ones. And when the difficult parts get neglected, the full systemic benefit isn't realized.

### Trap 2 — Starting before the organization is ready

A successful digital transformation project requires foundation: processes already standardized, data already reasonably clean, users with sufficient awareness, and leadership commitment that is strong and consistent.

Many projects begin when one or more of those conditions aren't ready — typically because of time pressure or insufficient assessment before starting.

The typical consequence: systems get installed on top of non-standardized processes, and then everyone discovers that processes need to be standardized — but now it has to happen while the system is already running, which is significantly more complex and risky than doing it beforehand.

### Trap 3 — Short-term result expectations from long-term change

Real digital transformation is long-term change — full value typically emerges after 1–3 years, not after 3–6 months.

But pressure from stakeholders (BOD, shareholders, management teams) often demands early visible results. And when short-term pressure is strong enough, project decisions get distorted: cutting the most important but least-immediately-visible phases (training, change management, data quality); accelerating toward go-live before readiness; or measuring ROI too early before value has had time to accumulate.

## An illustrative scenario — "Comprehensive digitalization project"

*This is a composite illustrative scenario drawn from common patterns. Not a case study of a specific organization.*

---

A processing business, 150 employees, decides to "digitalize everything" after the CEO attends an Industry 4.0 conference.

**Defined scope:** ERP + eQMS + DMS + warehouse management + production line integration. Timeline: 18 months. Budget: significant.

**Months 1–6: Kickoff and design**
Consultants engaged, processes analyzed, software selected. Discovery that many processes aren't documented — standardization required first. This wasn't in the original timeline. Timeline extended by 3 months.

**Months 7–12: Parallel implementation**
ERP and eQMS implementation begins simultaneously to "make up time." IT team and consultants managing 3–4 workstreams at once. Users receive training on multiple systems in a short window; many aren't proficient before go-live.

**Months 13–18: Extension and adjustment**
Several modules delayed — warehouse management and line integration pushed to "phase 2" with no clear timeline. The portions that went live (basic ERP and eQMS) are running but many employees still use Excel in parallel "because it's easier." Data quality in ERP is low due to rushed migration.

**Actual state at month 20:**
- ERP running at 40–50% of expected capability.
- eQMS used for audit documentation but not integrated into actual operational processes.
- Warehouse still on Excel.
- Line integration not started.
- Total cost 35–40% over original budget.

The project hasn't completely failed — some value was created. But the gap between initial expectations and actual results is significant.

## The alternative — not big bang, but progressive

Instead of "digitalize everything" in one project, a progressive approach works differently:

**Start from the problem, not from the transformation vision.**
The right question isn't "what kind of digital company do we want to be?" but "what is the most important operational problem we're experiencing — and what is the smallest step that could begin to address it?"

**Scope small enough to control, significant enough to create value.**
Each phase should be completable in 3–6 months, have measurable outcomes, and create the foundation for the next phase — not "stage 1 of a 3-year plan" with no clear interim value.

**Ensure foundation before expanding.**
If phase 1 isn't operating at the expected level, don't expand to phase 2. This sounds straightforward but requires discipline under pressure to "go faster."

**Measure and learn at every step.**
Each phase ends with a clear question: did it create the expected value? What did we learn? What should phase 2 be based on what we've learned?

---

## Conclusion

"Large digital transformation projects" don't always fail — and not every organization needs to avoid large-scale projects. But many failures are preventable by understanding the risk mechanisms and designing to reduce them from the start.

The question isn't "could this project fail?" — it's "have we designed for early problem detection? Does scope fit the organization's absorption capacity? Is the foundation ready for the first step?"
